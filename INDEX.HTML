Concept 1 — Informed Consent

Informed Consent = Patient agrees to treatment after being fully informed

  • The provider explains the procedure, risks, benefits, and alternatives
  • The patient has the chance to ask questions
  • Patient agrees voluntarily and knowingly
  • Usually required for surgery, invasive procedures, anesthesia
  • Documented in the medical record with a signed consent form
  • This is a clinical/legal concept — it is about agreeing to receive care

Concept 2 — Implied Consent

Implied Consent = Consent shown through actions, not explicit discussion

  • No verbal or written agreement is made — the patient's actions imply consent
  • Classic example: A patient extends their arm for a blood draw — no verbal agreement needed
  • Another example: A patient arrives for a scheduled appointment — implies consent for routine care
  • Applies to routine, low-risk procedures where the patient's cooperation makes agreement obvious
  • Does NOT apply to surgery, invasive procedures, or sensitive treatments — those need informed consent

Concept 3 — HIPAA Authorization

HIPAA Authorization = Specific written permission for PHI uses not covered by TPO

  • This is NOT the same as treatment consent
  • Required when PHI will be used or disclosed for purposes outside of Treatment, Payment, or Operations (TPO)
  • Examples needing authorization: marketing, research, sharing with employers, life insurance applications
  • Must include: patient name, description of info, who receives it, purpose, expiration date, patient signature, right to revoke
  • Key point: Treatment consent and HIPAA authorization are separate documents for separate purposes

Concept 5 — Use of PHI

USE = Sharing or accessing PHI WITHIN the organization

  • PHI stays inside the covered entity or its business associates
  • Examples: a biller accesses a patient's record to process a claim, a coder reviews documentation to assign codes, a compliance officer audits billing records
  • Information does not leave the organization
  • TPO activities within the organization = permitted uses
Concept 6 — Disclosure of PHI

DISCLOSURE = Sharing PHI OUTSIDE the organization

  • PHI is released to a party external to the covered entity
  • Examples: sending records to another provider, submitting a claim to an insurer, releasing records to a patient's attorney
  • Disclosures require valid justification — TPO, patient authorization, or legal requirement
  • Unauthorized disclosures = HIPAA violations

Concept 7 — Minimum Necessary Standard

Only access or share the minimum PHI needed for the specific purpose

  • Applies to both uses and disclosures
  • Staff should only access the portions of a record needed for their specific task
  • Example: A biller processing a claim does not need to read a patient's full psychiatric history
  • Example: A receptionist confirming an appointment does not need access to detailed lab results
  • Does NOT apply to: disclosures to providers for treatment, patients accessing their own records

Concept 8 — Safeguard Categories: Extended Examples

Administrative · Physical · Technical — What Does Each Look Like in Practice?

These three safeguard categories are required by the HIPAA Security Rule to protect electronic PHI:

📋

Administrative

People + Policy controls:

  • Workforce HIPAA training program
  • Designated Privacy/Security Officer
  • Risk analysis and risk management
  • Sanction policy for violations
  • Workforce clearance procedures
  • Contingency/disaster recovery planning
🔒

Physical

Facility + Hardware controls:

  • Locked server and data rooms
  • Badge/key card access control
  • Workstation screen positioning
  • Visitor sign-in and escort policies
  • Secure device disposal procedures
  • Surveillance of secure areas
💻

Technical

Software + System controls:

  • Unique user IDs and strong passwords
  • Data encryption (at rest + in transit)
  • Automatic session logoff (timeout)
  • Audit logs for all access events
  • Role-based access controls (RBAC)
  • Emergency access procedures

Concept 9 — Workforce Training

Training is required, ongoing, and documented

  • All workforce members who access PHI must receive HIPAA training
  • Training must occur at hire AND periodically after — not just once
  • Covered topics: privacy policies, security procedures, how to handle PHI, reporting violations
  • Training completion must be documented — records are reviewed during audits
  • Skipping training or failing to document it = administrative safeguard failure
  • New threats and policies require updated training

Concept 10 — Auditing & Access Log Monitoring

Auditing finds problems before they become major violations

  • Audit logs track who accessed what records, when, and from where
  • Routine review of logs can detect unauthorized or suspicious access
  • Example: A compliance officer notices an employee accessed 200 patient records they have no clinical reason to view → triggers investigation
  • Role-based access control (RBAC) limits what each staff member can see based on their job function
  • Audits support proactive compliance — catching issues before a breach occurs
  • Audit log review is a technical + administrative safeguard combined activity

Concept 11 — Proactive vs Reactive Compliance

Good compliance prevents violations — it doesn't just respond to them

  • Proactive: Risk assessments, ongoing training, routine access log reviews, policy updates, workforce sanction policies
  • Reactive: Investigating a breach after it occurs, notifying patients, reporting to HHS, paying fines
  • HIPAA requires proactive measures — offices must not wait for a breach to implement safeguards
  • The CBCS exam tests whether students can identify activities that prevent violations vs. those that respond to violations

💡 HIPAA Made Simple
What is HIPAA?1996 federal law protecting patient health information. Sets national privacy, security, and confidentiality standards.
Covered EntitiesHealthcare providers · Health plans · Healthcare clearinghouses. All must comply with HIPAA.
Business AssociatesThird parties handling PHI on behalf of covered entities. Must sign a Business Associate Agreement (BAA).
PHIIndividually identifiable health information. Includes name+diagnosis, SSN, account number, member ID, phone with health info.
De-identified dataNo identifiers present = NOT PHI. HIPAA does not apply to de-identified data.
TPOTreatment, Payment, Operations. PHI may be used/disclosed for these without separate authorization.

🤝 Consent & Authorization
Informed ConsentFull disclosure of risks, benefits, alternatives. Signed form. For surgery and invasive procedures. About treatment decisions.
Implied ConsentPatient's action communicates agreement. No signature needed. For routine, low-risk procedures (e.g., blood draw, blood pressure).
HIPAA AuthorizationSpecific written permission for NON-TPO uses/disclosures. Required for: marketing, research, employer requests, life insurance.
Key TrapTreatment consent ≠ HIPAA authorization. They are separate documents for separate purposes.
🔁 Use vs Disclosure
USEPHI shared or accessed WITHIN the covered entity. Internal. Billing dept, coders, compliance officer reviewing records = USE.
DISCLOSUREPHI released OUTSIDE the organization. External. Claims to insurer, records to specialist, records to attorney = DISCLOSURE.
Minimum NecessaryOnly access/share the minimum PHI needed for the task. Applies to uses and disclosures. Exception: treatment disclosures to providers.
📋 Release of Information Checklist
Before releasing, verify:Form is signed · Description of info is complete · Recipient is identified · Purpose stated · Expiration date present · Right to revoke noted
Incomplete form =DO NOT RELEASE. Hold and request correction. Never fill in missing fields yourself.
Unauthorized releaseReleasing PHI without valid authorization or legal basis = HIPAA violation regardless of intent.
Family membersAdult patients control their own PHI. Family does not automatically have access — written authorization required.
